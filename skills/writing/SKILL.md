# Writing

Weak writing, from people and models alike, usually fails the same way: it reaches for a familiar shape instead of saying the specific true thing. Good copy is built from facts. The rest of this skill follows from that.

## Start from the material

If `.agents/product-marketing.md` exists, read it first. It holds the product, audience, proof and voice.

Before drafting, know who is reading, what they already know and where they came from, the one thing they should do or believe afterwards, and what proof exists. Ask only for what you can't work out yourself.

## What makes it work

- **Clear over clever.** A line the reader has to decode has already lost them.
- **Specific over general.** Use a number, a name, a mechanism or a step. "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes" beats "save time."
- **The customer's words over the company's.** Reviews, tickets and interviews are the best source.
- **Benefits are features followed through.** Ask "so what?" until you reach something the reader cares about.
- **One idea per section.** The page should build an argument, not list things.
- **The swap test.** If a line would work unchanged on a competitor's site, it says nothing.

## Claims

Write only what the source material supports. Never invent stats, testimonials, customer names or guarantees, not even to fix a vague line. When a requested claim isn't supported, say so, offer the closest true version, and name the evidence that would unlock the original (a review export, a written policy, a citable study). Mark gaps inline as `[NEED: ...]`.

## Patterns that read as machine-written

A reader who spots one starts doubting the claims around it. The shape of a sentence gives it away more than its vocabulary. Fix these by rewriting from the facts. Swapping in synonyms only creates a new tell.

- **Contrast reveal.** "It's not X, it's Y." "Not because X. Because Y." "Not just X, but Y." Fix: state Y with its reason.
- **Negation list.** "No setup, no templates, no waiting." Fix: say what does happen. A single real absence next to the CTA ("No card required") is fine.
- **Trailing pile-on.** A complete claim, then a comma, then restatements or an "-ing" clause ("…, ensuring nothing slips"). Fix: end at the claim. A clause that adds a new fact can stay.
- **Self-answered question.** "The result? 3x faster." Fix: keep the answer. FAQs and questions in the reader's own voice ("Need to share a screenshot?") are fine.
- **Colon reveal.** "The best part: it learns." Fix: write it as a normal sentence.
- **Stock openers and closers.** "In today's fast-paced world," "Whether you're X or Y," "Here's the thing," "Say goodbye to," "Unlock the power of," "X, reimagined." Fix: cut them.
- **Puffery and weasel words.** "Pivotal," "redefines," "industry-leading," "experts agree," "trusted by leaders." Fix: name the source or cut it.
- **Placeholder adjectives.** Seamless, robust, powerful, innovative, streamline, empower. Fix: write the fact each one stands in for.
- **Decoration.** Hype punctuation, emoji as bullets, a bold label on every bullet, every bullet opening with the same verb form, Title Case headings, em dashes in headlines and other short copy.

Fragments, lists of three, rhetorical questions and em dashes are fine when you choose them on purpose. They become tells when they are the default, so use each at most once per section and for a reason.

Specific banned words go stale as models change. The structural patterns and the swap and specificity tests don't. Keep the brand's register: a formal sentence that makes a specific claim is fine, and flattening everything into clipped startup voice is its own tell. Vary your fixes too. If every fix turns into ", because…", that is the new tic.

## Editing someone else's text

Keep the author's message and voice. Change what's broken, and say why when it isn't obvious. Look for unclear sentences, features with no "so what," claims with no proof, vague phrases that could be a number, and objections left unanswered near the ask. Cut filler words (very, really, just, in order to), prefer plain verbs, and use the active voice.

## Delivering

Give the copy itself, ready to paste. For headlines and CTAs, offer a few genuinely different options, each with one line on what it bets on. Collect any `[NEED: ...]` items at the end so they can be acted on.
